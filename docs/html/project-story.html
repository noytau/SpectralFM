SpectralFM

Teaching a model to understand radio signals nobody has labeled

A self-supervised model that learns the structure of raw radio-telescope spectra without being told what any of it means — because almost none of it has been labeled, and manually labeling millions of signals isn't an option.

The problem

Millions of signals, almost no labels

The NOVA radio observatory produces an enormous volume of 1D spectral data — each sample a 245-point signal, the electromagnetic fingerprint of a brief moment of sky. Some of it has been labeled by hand for specific studies. The overwhelming majority hasn't, and never will be — there's simply too much of it.

That's a familiar problem in speech and language, where self-supervised learning let models absorb structure from raw audio or text long before anyone attached a label to it. SpectralFM is that approach, aimed at radio spectra instead.

The approach

Borrowing data2vec from speech, applied to spectra

The core model is data2vec-audio, an architecture built for self-supervised speech representation learning. SpectralFM repurposes it for signals that look nothing like speech: 245 samples instead of seconds of 16kHz audio, no phonemes, no words — just raw spectral shape.

Training has no labels in the loop at all. A small convolutional front-end compresses each 245-point signal down to a short sequence of feature vectors; a 12-layer transformer encodes them; and the model is trained to predict its own masked-out regions, checked against a slowly-updated copy of itself rather than any ground truth. If the model can fill in what it can't see, it has learned something real about the structure of the signal — this is the whole premise of self-supervised learning, applied to a domain where hand-labeling was never going to scale.

raw signal → conv feature extractor → transformer → predict what's masked

What's been built

A backbone, an evaluation suite, and a second line of experiments

Beyond the core training pipeline, three things now exist:

9.1M
signals in the largest training set
7
evaluation methods, from embedding similarity to label regression
3
reconstruction decoder heads, testing what survives at each depth

Since the backbone never sees a label during training, judging whether it actually learned anything useful requires a dedicated evaluation pipeline — measuring things like whether similar signals end up with similar embeddings, whether the model is robust to noise, and whether its representations are predictive of the handful of labels that do exist. Alongside that, a second, newer line of work bolts reconstruction decoders onto a trained backbone, testing how much of the original signal survives at each stage of compression — the autoencoder work.

What we've learned

The clearest result so far is a negative one

The evaluation suite surfaced a finding that's now steering the next round of work more than anything else:

Reconstruction ≠ representation

Backbones trained to reconstruct the original signal well turn out to not be the ones whose embeddings are useful downstream. The original self-supervised backbone — trained purely to predict its own masked regions, never explicitly to reconstruct anything — is the only one so far whose embeddings are meaningfully predictive of real labels. Every reconstruction-trained variant tested scores close to zero on that same measure, even though it reconstructs the signal more faithfully.

In other words: a model that's good at rebuilding what it saw isn't automatically a model that understood what it saw. That's an unintuitive result worth taking seriously, and it's currently shaping the plan — rather than training a backbone to reconstruct signals directly, the more promising direction is adding reconstruction decoders on top of a backbone that was already trained the original, purely self-supervised way, and keeping that backbone frozen.

Where this goes next

More systematic sweeps, not more raw scale

The largest training runs so far have already used most of the available single-channel data. The open questions now are less about scale and more about design: which parts of the model should be frozen versus trained, how the reconstruction decoders should be weighted against each other, and whether the same "reconstruction isn't representation" pattern holds once decoders are layered onto a frozen, already-good backbone instead of training through it.
