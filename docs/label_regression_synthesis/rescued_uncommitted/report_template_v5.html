SpectralFM · label-regression evaluation

Does the embedding give a foundation for few-shot label regression?

On the Feb-25 SSL data2vec-audio backbone and the NOVA parameter_0 regression target: does a frozen embedding beat correctly-probed raw input, at any label budget from 10 to the full 4,716-spectrum pool? Answer, method, and the evidence, current as of this report's last measurement pass.

Bottom line/ Method/ Signal across the pipeline/ Recipe search/ Label efficiency/ The PLS/float64 check/ Why raw wins/ Recommendations/ Methodology & validity
NO
Raw input beats the frozen embedding at every label budget measured, n=10 through the entire 3,716-spectrum training pool, and across every one of 456 extraction recipes searched. There is no n at which the embedding wins. The single biggest lever measured anywhere in this study is whitening (decorrelating and rescaling the feature space to unit variance) — it helps raw input more than it helps the embedding, because raw input had more untapped structure to begin with. The binding constraint is the representation, not the label budget: collecting more labels will not close this gap on the data in hand.
Method

One protocol, five recipes, a pre-registered win rule

Every comparison in this report follows the same discipline: a win rule fixed and unit-tested before any number was measured, two disjoint 500-spectrum evaluation sets drawn from the 4,716-spectrum labeled corpus (eval_a for recipe selection, eval_b for reporting — a recipe never gets to pick itself a winning test set), and paired draws so two arms are always compared on identical training samples at a given n. The remaining 3,716 spectra are the training pool the label-efficiency curves below draw from.

Four representations are compared: raw input (245-point spectrum), raw input, z-scored per-sample, the frozen embedding at its best extraction recipe found by search (layer × pooling × normalization), and the embedding at its historical tap (final transformer layer, mean-pooled — the recipe every earlier measurement of this backbone used). A probe panel spanning linear (OLS, Ridge, RidgeCV), kernel (RBF-GP, RBF-KRR), graph-propagation, k-NN, and PLS regressors is run for every recipe at every n, and only the best-of-panel result is reported — TabPFN was tested throughout and is excluded from every "best" selection (see the note in Recipe search below).

Recipe search

456 recipes, one winner: whitened raw input

Every transformer depth (13 layers) × six poolings × four unlabeled-corpus normalizations (a recipe) was screened on eval_a and confirmed on the disjoint eval_b, under the win rule fixed before any number was measured. Result: 0 of 6 confirmation cells favor the embedding at n∈{20,50}, bootstrap P(Δ>0)=0.00 in every cell.

The confirmation grid, held-out set. Raw (orange) leads in every one of the six pre-registered cells.

The most reusable finding in this study. The final transformer layer — the tap every earlier measurement of this backbone used — is among the worst taps. The peak sits at blocks 0–3, roughly 2–2.5× stronger. This changes the measurement basis for every embedding claim on this backbone, including the checkpoint ranking that guides the training programme.

What "whitening" does

Whitening is fitted once on the 4,716 unlabeled spectra (no labels involved) and rescales the feature space so its covariance becomes the identity matrix — every direction decorrelated from every other and brought to unit variance, instead of a few directions quietly dominating a probe's regularization.

A 2-D stand-in for what happens in 245 (raw) or up to 768 (embedding) real dimensions. Before whitening (left), the cloud is an elongated ellipse. After whitening (right), it's round — covariance = identity. One numerical detail: at this sample size, whitening the full 768-dim embedding is unstable, so it's capped at the top 256 directions (~18 samples/direction), matching the 245-dim raw input's natural scale.

A parallel-branch signal, checked here

PLS reaches a much higher raw-input ceiling at large n — it does not help at few-shot n

A separate study on this same corpus found that raw input's true large-n ceiling is far higher than a RidgeCV-based probe panel reaches: at the full n=4,716, a PLS regressor fitted honestly inside each cross-validation fold (verified leak-free by a shuffled-label canary, and stable whether computed in float32 or float64) scores 0.825 at 1-component, against RidgeCV's 0.404 — because RidgeCV's isotropic shrinkage penalizes the near-degenerate, highly collinear directions where raw input's signal actually lives, and PLS selects directions by label-covariance instead, reaching it directly. That finding does not change this report's own conclusion — the whitened-raw numbers already measured above (0.856 at the full pool) meet or exceed it, because whitening addresses the same collinearity problem by a different route.

What was untested was whether this same lever pays off in the few-shot regime this report is about (n≤50) — PLS was never part of the small-n probe panel here. Checked directly: a fold-internal PLS probe (k swept small enough to fit safely at these sample sizes), shuffled-label canary run at every cell (always cleanly negative — no leak, e.g. −0.37 to −0.70 across cells where the real signal reaches up to +0.59).

Conclusion: the float64/PLS ceiling lift is real, but it is a large-n phenomenon that does not transfer to this report's few-shot decision regime. It does not change the recommendation to ship whitening, and it is not a substitute for it at small n.

Why

The mechanism: dilution, not absence

The label signal is present in the embedding — the pipeline sweep shows most of it is linearly recoverable at large n. But it is spread across many collinear directions, and at 10–2,000 labels only a handful of them can be reliably estimated. Raw input's 245 dimensions are physically meaningful axes that already concentrate the signal, and every generic lever tried here — whitening, PLS, kernel methods — helps raw more than it helps the embedding, because raw had more untapped structure to begin with.

A 245-point smooth 1-D signal with a smooth physical target is close to the worst case for demonstrating foundation-model value: raw features are already near-optimal and low-dimensional, so there is no representation gap for a foundation model to close. That is a property of this task, not evidence that the backbone failed at what it was trained to do.

Recommendations

Where the remaining leverage is

  1. Re-measure the reconstruction-trained checkpoints under the whitened-recipe protocol.

    Their existing scores were taken at the historical layer12/mean tap — shown here to be among the worst available. The checkpoint ranking guiding the training programme is currently unverified against a fair recipe.

  2. Ship whitening as part of the deployed pipeline, not as a post-hoc probe choice.

    It is the single largest lever measured in this study (+0.47 R² at 1-comp/n=50) and is fit label-free on the unlabeled corpus, so it costs nothing extra to deploy. Supervised compression (PLS) is not a substitute at few-shot n — checked directly above, it underperforms the existing panel at n≤50 on the whitened recipe. It has a real, separately-verified benefit only once n is large (near the full corpus).

  3. Test active or stratified label selection.

    Every draw in this study was random. Selection is a geometry problem — exactly where an embedding's structure, even if its raw features don't win, could still add value.

  4. Re-aim the foundation-model demonstration at a task where raw features actually fail.

    Multi-component disentangling, anomaly detection, or cross-instrument transfer — not single-component regression on a smooth, low-dimensional, pre-standardized signal.

Methodology & validity

How this negative result is protected against being wrong

A pre-registered win rule, written and unit-tested before any number was measured; two disjoint evaluation sets (rank on one, report on the other); paired draws so both arms are compared on identical training samples; and a shuffled-label canary run wherever a supervised transform (PLS, in particular) enters the pipeline, to catch a label leak before it can inflate a result. All of this machinery is covered by regression tests in the merged code.
